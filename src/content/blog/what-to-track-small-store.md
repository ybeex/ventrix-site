When people first decide to get organised, they usually try to track everything. Every screw, every glove, every pencil. Within three weeks, the system is abandoned, because nobody has time to log a single washer.

The answer isn't to track nothing. It's to track the right things, properly, and ignore the rest on purpose.

## The principle: track what's expensive to lose

Tracking has a cost: the time it takes at the counter, plus the effort of keeping the record accurate. It's worth it only when that cost is smaller than the cost of losing track.

Ask three questions about any item:

1. **How much does it cost to replace?** Not just the price, but the delay.
2. **What happens if it's missing when needed?** Does work stop? Is it a safety issue?
3. **How likely is it to go missing?** Small, portable, desirable items walk off.

Anything that scores high on any of these is a candidate for tracking.

## A simple sorting method

### Track individually (assets)

Items where *which one* matters:

- Power tools and expensive hand tools
- Test and measuring equipment (especially calibrated)
- Laptops, tablets, cameras, projectors
- Ladders, safety harnesses, anything with a safety inspection
- Keys and access cards

Give each a number or tag, a photo, a home shelf, and check it out to a person.

### Track by quantity (consumables)

Items where *how many* matters, and running out hurts:

- Items that stop work when they run out (specific fasteners, gaskets, filters)
- Items with long supplier lead times
- Items that are costly in bulk (consumables with real money in them: cables, solvents, welding rod)

Give each a unit, a minimum and a reorder quantity.

### Don't track (or track as a bulk bin)

Items too cheap, too common or too easy to replace:

- Loose nuts, washers, cable ties
- Pens, tape, cleaning cloths
- Anything you can replace the same day for pocket change

For these, use a "two-bin" or visual rule: when the first bin is empty, reorder, and use the second. No logging required.

## The 80/20 check

In many stores, a small share of items accounts for most of the value and most of the headaches. Before you add anything, list your twenty most valuable or most-often-missing items. Track those first, properly. You'll probably find that this gives you most of the benefit with a fraction of the work.

## Group when it makes sense

Several identical low-value tools can be tracked as one line with a quantity ("Hex key set, qty 8") instead of eight records. Do this only when you don't care *which* one someone has.

## Add detail slowly

Start with the minimum: a name, a unit, a home, a type. Add minimums, photos and notes later, when you notice you need them. A simple record that's kept up beats a rich one that's abandoned.

## Review twice a year

Which items did you track but never needed? Stop. Which kept going missing? Start tracking them. Your list should change as your store does.

## A starter checklist

- [ ] List 20 items you can't afford to lose or run out of
- [ ] Decide for each: asset or consumable
- [ ] Give assets a tag and a home; give consumables a unit and minimum
- [ ] Set up check-out for assets and low-stock alerts for consumables
- [ ] Review after 30 days and adjust
